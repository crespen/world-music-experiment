# Earth to Ear — Musical Journeys Editorial Standard

**Working guidance · November 11, 2026**  
**Purpose:** Invite visitors to spend time *with* music rather than treating an enormous library as a checklist. The listener may encounter something new, connect it with memory or heritage, or follow a creative idea through generations. Participation and reflection remain optional.

## Focus beats encyclopedic breadth

Start with **one identifiable musical style or practice, in one grounded place or cultural setting, across an evidence-supported stretch of time**.

Examples:
- Not “the history of jazz,” but “jazz vocal phrasing and improvisation in the United States, from the swing era to contemporary interpreters.”
- Not “all Ghanaian music,” but “guitar-band / palm-wine highlife in Ghana and how its performance changed.”
- Not “Mexican folk music,” but “son jarocho in Veracruz, including commercial recording, the fandango revival and newer practice.”
- Not “Asian music,” but “a documented path through Balinese gong kebyar recordings and performance.”

The unit can be a country, region, diaspora network or community. Some musical traditions cross these boundaries; acknowledge that honestly instead of forcing geography into an inaccurate border.

## Is this an actual journey?

Publish an **across-time journey** only where we can identify:
1. **A coherent thread**: one musical technique, ensemble type, singing approach, genre subdivision, practice or sound.
2. **At least three meaningfully different points**, typically early, middle and recent; a pair of bookends alone omits consequential developments.
3. **Real, source-linked examples** that listeners can hear, ideally from original artist, archival or broadcaster pages; older material may be short previews.
4. **Documented change and continuity**: describe what carried through, what changed, and who made important interventions. Avoid a fake one-way evolution or a claim of direct influence based only on similar sounds.
5. **A listening invitation**, not a lesson, exam or value judgment. Readers may start from any point in the story and work in either direction.
6. **Cultural care**: music may be rooted in community practice, social change or migration; be clear about what is known and leave speculative personal/ancestral associations to the listener.

If sufficient historical audio or evidence is not available, keep the concept as a **thematic listening conversation** or an **interview** rather than presenting a made-up century-long timeline. No obligation to cover every genre or nation.

## Three optional listening doorways

**Encounter:** “What caught your attention?”  
**Connection:** “Did this remind you of anything—someone, somewhere, a part of your life?”  
**Journey:** “What changed between these performances, and what stayed familiar?”

No quiz, required ratings, forced journaling, biological/ancestry assumptions, cultural stereotyping, or pop-up on every login. The private contributor journal is by invitation; browsing and listening are public. A first-visit introduction should be easy to dismiss and revisit later as **How to Listen**.

## Standard editorial package

- **Working title / specific style / location / central question.**
- **3–8 listening anchors**, with recording date versus musical era carefully separated.
- A **concise narrative** (not a rigid word-count quota), ideally with an individual whose contribution is often overlooked.
- **What changed / what persisted / what remains contested**.
- **One optional reflective question**.
- **Further sources** and caveats; link to source audio, never imply that a track was rehosted or is perpetually free.
- **Related journeys** and a possible podcast guest.

Keep articles, audio episodes and catalog recordings linked, but independently enjoyable. Historical examples do not need to be added to the public catalog merely to pitch an article.
