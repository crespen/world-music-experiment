# SOUTH AMERICAN MUSICAL ATLAS

**Earth to Ear | A Global Listening Experiment**

**Volume III · Edition v0.2 · November 13, 2026**  
**Status:** Working continent-wide research inventory. Not a verified distinct-genre census or Listening Library playlist.  
**Companion volumes:** [North American Musical Atlas v0.3](north-american-musical-atlas-v0-3.md) and [African Musical Atlas v0.3](african-musical-atlas-v0-3.md). **Method:** [Global Classification Standard v1.0](../atlas-classification-standard-v1.md).

This first edition maps musical diversity across **all twelve sovereign South American countries**, **French Guiana**, and a separate territorial research note for the **Falkland Islands / Islas Malvinas**. Nation and territory names are navigational; they do not imply exclusive cultural ownership. This is a *research inventory*, not a competition over the number of genres in any country.

## Research design and classification rules

**Global rule:** Primary historical lens = period and mechanism of formation, not whether the music remains alive. [G]/[F]/[P]/[S]/[A]/[R] are the same type codes applied in the Africa and North America v0.3 inventories. Each term has one primary lens; later reinterpretations can be linked separately. See the [global standard](../atlas-classification-standard-v1.md).

- **Three historical lenses:** *Living and inherited traditions*, *historical transformations*, and *contemporary expressions*. Each is a listening-era/context lens, not a claim that living music is unchanged or that historical genres are dead. A tradition can qualify for several lenses; it is listed where its analytical significance is most useful.
- **Typed entries:** **G** is a genre/named style; **F** a song or musical-poetic form; **P** a performance, ritual, dance or ensemble practice; **S** a scene or movement; **A** a composed/concert-music tradition; **R** an unresolved research lead. Different types must not be counted as distinct musical genres without review.
- **Comparable curiosity, not numerical quotas:** Brazil or Argentina need not have the same number of entries as Guyana or Paraguay. However, each country should receive comparable *research attention* and the same three historical lenses.
- **Sacred and composed music everywhere:** Devotional, church, Indigenous, Afro-descendant, Indigenous-Christian, public ceremonial, orchestral and experimental composition all belong in the research agenda. A source does not establish public performance permission.
- **Music first when curating recordings:** A credible UNESCO or academic documentary is cultural evidence, not a default listening selection. The Atlas inventories musical entities; any new site recording must pass the separate editorial publishing gate before appearing in the Listening Library.
- **Sources and uncertainty:** Bracketed source IDs at the country section link to institutional, academic or specialist starting points. They establish *relevance*, not item-by-item authentication. Many scene descriptions and community-specific labels require direct follow-up before promotion to canonical tags.

## 15 | Cross-border musical families and evidence questions

- **Río de la Plata: Argentina ↔ Uruguay.** Tango, milonga, payada and related urban/rural practices; document how port cities, migration, recordings and national heritage narratives interact. **Do not assign a single-country origin by default.** (Research anchors: S02,S03,S04.)
- **Guaraní basin: Paraguay ↔ northeastern Argentina ↔ southern Brazil.** Polca paraguaya, guarania and chamamé circulate among neighboring regions, but are not synonyms and arose under differing histories. Guarania has a documented 1925 compositional origin. (Research anchors: S02,S22.)
- **Andean plateau: Bolivia ↔ Peru ↔ Chile ↔ Argentina.** Aymara/Quechua community celebrations, sikuri ensembles, huayno, panpipes and dance ensembles. Community identity and local variants matter more than modern borders. (Research anchors: S19,S25.)
- **Pacific marimba: Colombia ↔ Ecuador.** Marimba, currulao and associated choral/devotional performance communities. Distinguish shared practices, source provenance and documented regional differences. (Research anchors: S12,S13,S14.)
- **Llanos / Orinoco: Colombia ↔ Venezuela.** Joropo, pasaje and related llanero practices. Styles change with locality, instrument choice and performance occasion. (Research anchors: S13,S32.)
- **Guianas Maroon corridor: Suriname ↔ French Guiana ↔ Guyana.** Aluku, Ndyuka and other Maroon musical histories, Awasa/Susa and later aléké. The historical archive may place a recording in several locations. (Research anchors: S28,S36,S37.)
- **South American mainland–Caribbean sea routes.** Calypso, chutney, soca, steelpan, reggae, salsa, cumbia and Afro-Caribbean sounds connect northern South America with islands, indenture and migration; ask *which documented route* rather than asserting generic “Caribbean influence.” (Research anchors: S01,S16,S17.)
- **Colombian cumbia and later regional reinventions.** Distinguish early Colombian cumbia forms from Peruvian chicha/cumbia, Chilean cumbia, Argentine cumbia villera and other localized developments; source origin and scene dates separately. (Research anchors: S12,S25.)
- **African diaspora and Atlantic ports.** Brazilian samba/choro/Maracatu, Río de la Plata candombe, Afro-Pacific marimba, Afro-Peruvian coastal styles and Maroon music share Atlantic-slavery contexts but are **not presumed one direct genre lineage**. (Research anchors: S01,S07,S12,S26,S28.)
- **Indian and Javanese indenture ↔ Afro-Caribbean exchange.** Guyana and Suriname: baithak gana, chowtaal, tassa, Javanese gamelan, chutney and later dance music; compare patronage, language and interethnic popular forms. (Research anchors: S16,S17,S28,S31.)
- **Indigenous modernity.** Contemporary Indigenous rap, pop, electronic and concert composition must be studied as living creation, not a museum afterthought. Record exact community and language. (Research anchors: S09,S25,S27.)

### Cross-volume links (classification as research relationships, not shared-origin assumptions)

- **[documented circulation under review]** Cumbia: Colombian and other South American developments ↔ Mexican, Central American and Caribbean scenes in [North America](north-american-musical-atlas-v0-3.md); map particular recordings and dates before making a directed influence claim.
- **[multiway Atlantic exchange]** Brazil ↔ Angola and Cabo Verde in [Africa](african-musical-atlas-v0-3.md): compare diaspora, migration, recorded circulation and popular dance-music exchanges without inferring a single straight lineage.
- **[shared community / Atlantic circulation]** Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana ↔ Caribbean regions in [North America](north-american-musical-atlas-v0-3.md): distinguish Maroon, Indo-Caribbean, Creole, and later urban networks.
- **[cultural geography]** Rapa Nui ↔ Oceania; its Chilean political location does not erase its Polynesian history. Reciprocal Oceania entry is a required future volume item, as with Hawaiʻi.

## 17 | Composed, sacred and concert music — continent-wide research register

**Composed music is a field of music-making, not a single European genre.** Research scored orchestral and chamber works, urban concert repertoires, improvisational ensembles, electroacoustic art, sacred choral composition and theater/film composition alongside popular and community music. Composer examples are *people*, not genres: Alberto Ginastera (Argentina), Alberto Villalpando (Bolivia), Heitor Villa-Lobos (Brazil), Luis Humberto Salgado and Gerardo Guevara (Ecuador), Agustín Barrios Mangoré and later guitar interpreters (Paraguay), Antonio Estévez and Evencio Castellanos (Venezuela). A composer’s nationality or use of a musical motif does not automatically establish the origin of that motif.

**Sacred research:** Catholic and Protestant choral music, Afro-Brazilian Candomblé/Umbanda, Afro-Guyanese Cumfa and Sankey practice, Indo-Guyanese and Indo-Surinamese devotional genres, Maroon ceremonial music, Indigenous ceremonial performances and hybrid Catholic-Indigenous festivals demand distinct contextual treatment. For restricted or community-controlled music, obtain community permission and appropriate cultural interpretation before suggesting publication.

**Source checks:** See [S20](#s20), [S21](#s21), [S24](#s24), [S31](#s31), [S33](#s33) for art-music research starts; community-specific ritual sources are not implicit playback permissions.

## 18 | Editorial evidence and open questions

1. **Normalize terms:** one canonical term ID per documented entity; preserve diacritics, spelling variants, local-language self-description, aliases, and contested taxonomy.
2. **Separate genre from context:** a song form, instrument, annual festival, singer, scene, dance and musical tradition are not automatically interchangeable.
3. **Check history before arrows:** distinguish documented circulation, shared practice, analogous rhythm, borrowing, recorded adoption and unsupported speculation.
4. **Broaden source mix:** archives and government registers, ethnomusicology, community-run collections, artist/label histories, contemporary local media and festival research, including underrepresented speakers and regions.
5. **Document community agency:** Indigenous and Maroon source provenance, sacred/restricted access, performer consent and whether source context has been altered by an archive or later editor.
6. **Do not promote a documentary to the listening library:** only an identifiable performance with sufficiently uninterrupted music, accurate source and independently confirmed start/content belongs in the primary listening lane. If uncertain, hold for review.
7. **Field-check doubtful candidates:** each G/F/P/S/A tag is provisional; R labels are expressly unapproved. Attach a source and a type-resolution decision before reporting a distinct validated-genre count.
