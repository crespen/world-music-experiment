Companion volumes: [North America v0.3](north-american-musical-atlas-v0-3.md), [South America v0.2](south-american-musical-atlas-v0-2.md).  
**Basis:** Africa v0.1 and *Musical Atlas Review: Africa and North America v0.1* (November 12, 2026), plus selected institutional checks.

The country and territory inventory preserves the breadth of the first edition and corrects its two-bucket problem. Entries are still candidate terms, **not** a tally of verified distinct genres. Borders are navigational; musical cultures and trade routes do not stop at borders. The African Union geographic structure remains the organizing reference.

## Editorial approach and v0.3 classification

**Rule:** Each country candidate is typed [G], [F], [P], [S], [A], or [R] and placed once by its formative development rather than whether it is still performed. Sacred and composed repertoires receive full consideration, without inventing works where the source record is incomplete. See [global standard](../atlas-classification-standard-v1.md).

- **Three working listening-era categories.** Living and inherited traditions; historical transformations; contemporary expressions. These describe research contexts, not a linear ladder: a community practice can be alive, historically rooted, and contemporary simultaneously.
- **Classification is typed.** Genre, scene, musical form, instrument, technique, sacred/ceremonial practice, artist and cross-border link are different entities. A UNESCO cultural inscription does not make an urban popular genre a village tradition.
- **Equal curiosity, not equal numerical quotas.** Country sections receive comparable research attention; we do not expand one nation's micro-genres while reducing others to a single generic label.
- **Sacred and composed music belong everywhere.** Include art/concert and religious music with the same care as popular or community music, subject to community permissions.
- **Music-first catalog rule.** A reference film is NOT the default recording. Prefer exact authorized, substantially uninterrupted musical performances. Documentary-first entries need separate musical-segment verification and must be labeled as research context until suitable listening is confirmed.
- **Evidence and caution.** This is an editorial reclassification of candidate names plus reviewer-identified research leads; individual entries do not become source-verified by being included in the atlas.

## Geographic inventory counts — not distinct genres

Each candidate bullet is counted once, even if it bundles several related named variants. [A] composed research slots without named repertory are **excluded** from these totals. These numbers are not a census of independently verified musical genres or publicly accessible recordings.

### Paths to listen for across African histories

- Contemporary city scenes: Lagos, Accra, Dakar, Kinshasa, Dar es Salaam, Johannesburg

### Research questions, not claims of direct lineage

- What changed when instruments were amplified?
- Which musical ideas traveled through trade and migration?
- What is called the same genre name but differs locally?
- What happened when state radio and labels standardized a performance?
- Who is credited in the archives and who is missing?
- Which musical practices are public and which require community authorization?


**Canary Islands research note:** Investigate Guanche/Amazigh historical connections, tajaraste drumming and dance. *Silbo gomero* is a whistled language and should not be tagged as a music genre; its sound and communication context may be relevant to a comparative research article.

## 10 | Composed, sacred and concert-music research register

### African composed concert and art music: cross-country research register

This is a **major field of music**, not a European-only category. Include scored concert works, composed choral repertories, religious compositions, and concert-performance traditions where the sources support the classification. Candidate composer case studies: **Ephraim Amu** (Ghana), **Fela Sowande** and **Akin Euba** (Nigeria), **Bongani Ndodana-Breen** (South Africa). These are people, not genres. Locate compositions and authorized performance recordings before making them primary listening selections.

### Sacred and devotional music: cross-country research register

Musical practice in African churches, mosques, traditional religious settings, and independent religious movements requires consistent treatment. Include public Coptic, Orthodox, Islamic devotional, choral, gospel, Shembe/Nazarite, Zionist, and other local traditions where public performance rights and contextual appropriateness can be established. Do not infer that a ritual is public merely because an archive contains video.
